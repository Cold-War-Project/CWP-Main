# Reaction events

A reusable class of small events: when something major happens elsewhere, the people of other countries react, and the government chooses how to answer. Jack's brief (2026-11-18): help the player feel the world, shape the forces of history with small lever pushes sized from what really happened, and make the effect differ by country and camp. Branch `feat/reaction-events`, worktree `~/forge/worktrees/reactions`, namespace `cwp_rx`, prefix `cwp_rx_`.

## Rules for this class

- **Every reaction event is a real choice** (Jack, 2026-11-18). Never "X happened, so Y happens to you". Two options at least, and neither is free: one kind of bad against another kind of bad is fine.
- **Mechanics only.** A reaction fires because the simulation produced the happening (a variable or global the owning content sets, or plain game state such as a country ceasing to be a subject). No dates, and the reaction never forces the next step of history: it pushes levers that the simulation then uses.
- **Small levers.** Interest-group approval +-2 or +-3 for five decaying years; the communist appeal modifier that the Cold War layer's movements read (+-5% to 20%, decaying; the economic aid pact gives -25% while it runs); Red Scare fear through `cwp_rs_move_fear` (+3 to +10, where the JE is open); colonial liberty desire +-0.05 to 0.15 a week for 2.5 years (the anti-colonial tide gives 0.2 per tier); relations +-10 to 20; weekly expenses of about 1.3% of GDP a year for a rearmament program (`cwp_rx_cost_rearmament`), a third of that for smaller commitments.
- **Legible.** Every lever is a named modifier from the literature (Emergency Regulations, China White Paper, Nuclear Umbrella, Constitutional Advance), so the cause shows in tooltips. The communist appeal modifier also shows on the Cold War map tooltip.
- **No spam.** Each happening fires once. Audiences are narrow (see the table). A country that has just had a reaction event skips the lesser ones for 180 days (`cwp_rx_recent`); the camp leader's own events are exempt.

## How it hooks in

`common/on_actions/cwp_reactions_on_actions.txt` chains `cwp_rx_detect` into `on_monthly_pulse`. The detector watches state the owning threads already set and fires the matching `cwp_rx_fire_*` effect (`common/scripted_effects/cwp_reaction_effects.txt`) the first month it sees it. On the first pulse it marks whatever is already true (the American bomb, anything already happened in a loaded save) as old news. Owners need not change anything; a thread that wants a reaction at once can call the `cwp_rx_fire_*` effect itself with the scopes it documents and set the matching `cwp_rx_<key>_done` global.

The camp of each country comes from the Cold War layer's monthly cache (`cwp_cw_in_west`, `cwp_cw_in_east`, `cwp_cw_west_leader`).

## The history behind the levers

Checked 2026-11-18 against the sources below; where a lever rests on judgement rather than a source it says so.

- **The Soviet bomb (August 1949) hardened the West and fed the communist peace campaign.** Truman announced the test on 23 September 1949; the H-bomb decision followed on 31 January 1950 against the advice of the AEC's General Advisory Committee (so: army for, scientists against), Fuchs was arrested days earlier, and NSC-68 (April 1950) asked for a large military build-up. The bomb is widely tied to the intensifying Red Scare (fear). A US embassy cable expected "widespread psychological repercussions, especially in Western Europe" and pressure for UN atomic control. The communist side exploited it through the Stockholm Appeal (March 1950), a petition to outlaw atomic weapons that drew far beyond party members; Western governments treated it as a front campaign. So the bomb **does** both radicalize anti-communists in the West and embolden communists elsewhere, but the second effect ran through peace campaigns, not revolution: hence a modest appeal push the government can suppress at a cost. The vote-share effect on Western European parties was not measured in what I found (judgement).
- **"Who lost China?"** The communist victory was "widely viewed within the United States as a catastrophe"; the China lobby and McCarthy blamed State Department China specialists (Lattimore, the "China hands"), and the administration answered with the China White Paper (August 1949), whose letter of transmittal says the outcome was "beyond the control" of the US. Both answers cost: investigations damaged the foreign service for years, the white paper was attacked as a whitewash.
- **Asia after 1949.** The State Department expected communist gains in China to "stimulate Communist movements throughout the area"; Mao's China armed the Viet Minh from 1950, the Malayan Communist Party's insurgency was under way, and governments answered with emergency powers (Malaya's Emergency Regulations, 1948) or by recognizing Beijing (Burma and India, December 1949). Hence the two options of `cwp_rx.11`. The Nationalist-victory mirror (`cwp_rx.12`) is counterfactual: modelled on communist parties after defeat elsewhere (Greece, 1949), demoralized and splitting (judgement).
- **Korea (June 1950)** turned containment military: NSC-68 adopted, Western European rearmament (Britain's programme of 1951 split the Labour cabinet over health charges, hence unions against), a defense boom for industry. Commodity booms (the Australian and New Zealand wool boom, Japan's special procurement) are real but left for a later slice.
- **Independence as example.** Nkrumah took Gandhi's methods and India's independence as a model; Ghana's independence (1957) was followed by a wave in Africa. The colonial powers answered with constitutional advance or emergencies (Malaya, Kenya). Hence colony and metropole options.
- **Berlin (1948-49)** backfired on the communists: contemporaries reported the Western European parties' offensive "didn't come off", and the airlift convinced Western leaders that a permanent alliance was needed. The appeal lever is negative in both options for that reason; the choice is money versus standing with the Western leader.
- **NATO (1949)**: the French and Italian communist parties campaigned against the Atlantic Pact, and in 1950 French dockers refused to unload American military aid shipments (judgement on magnitude).
