# Research brief: rocketry and space programmes, 1945-2026 (companion to the space programme design)

Research sub-agent, 2026-11-22. Written before the design text existed: it checks the designer's 32 statements of memory and supplies starting conditions, numbers and dates.

**Certainty marks**

| Mark | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **C** | confirmed this session in two or more good sources, or in one official history or primary document fetched this session |
| **C\*** | a date of record that the standard reference works give identically; not re-fetched this session. Safe for design, re-check before it is quoted in event text |
| **P** | probable: one good source, or sources differ on a detail (the difference is stated). **P (nf)** = from the standard literature, not fetched this session |
| **D** | disputed among historians, or doubtful. The dispute is stated |

Source numbers in square brackets refer to section 7. WP = English Wikipedia, used as a pointer to what it cites. "Computed" = worked out here from a standard formula, shown so it can be re-done.

**Covered:** all 32 statements (section 1); a 26-row table of the world on 1 January 1946 (section 2); a timeline of about 100 dates (section 3); how programmes were decided and cut (section 4); myths (section 5).

**Not covered, or covered thinly:** no book was read in full. Siddiqi's *Challenge to Apollo* (NASA SP-2000-4408), Chertok's *Rockets and People*, McDougall, Logsdon, Launius's polling article and Harvey's national histories are cited from memory of their contents or through web summaries: every statement resting on them is marked P (nf) or C\*. Russian, Chinese, Japanese, French and Spanish sources were read only through English summaries (two Spanish and one French page excepted). Cost figures for the Soviet Union and China are estimates by outsiders. Section 6 lists what could not be confirmed.

**Dates have two values more often than expected.** Many events fall on different calendar days in UTC and in local time (Explorer 1, Luna 2, Starfish Prime, the third N1, Mir, Xichang 1996, the first Falcon 9 landing, Chang'e 6). The table gives both where it matters. Pick one convention for the mod (local date at the launch site is what newspapers of the day printed).

**Living people.** Flagged *(living)* at first mention where a game text might name them. ## 4. How programmes were really decided, and why they stalled

**4.1 What made governments start.** Four motives recur, usually together.
*Missiles.* Almost every first national launcher was a ballistic missile or its sibling: the R-7, Redstone/Jupiter-C, Thor, Atlas, China's Long March 1, Israel's Shavit, North Korea's Unha, Iran's Safir. The exceptions are instructive: Vanguard (deliberately non-military, and late), Japan's university-built solid rockets (kept unguided at first to stay clear of missile work), Britain's Black Arrow (from a re-entry test vehicle). A country with a missile programme gets a launcher almost free; a country without one pays the full price. This is also why the MTCR (1987) stopped launchers in Brazil, Argentina and South Africa [12] (P nf).
*Prestige, usually after a shock.* The Soviet leadership did not foresee Sputnik's effect and was surprised by it (P nf, Siddiqi). Kennedy's Moon decision followed Gagarin by six weeks and the Bay of Pigs by five; his memorandum to the vice-president of 20 April 1961 asks for "a space program which promises dramatic results in which we could win" (C\*, re-check wording before quoting). On a White House tape of 21 November 1962 he tells NASA's chief "I'm not that interested in space" (C\*, re-check). Prestige bought the programme; it did not buy lasting public support (item 10).
*Alliance politics.* Guest seats on Soyuz and the Shuttle were handed out in the same years by both blocs (1.15). Britain offered its cancelled Blue Streak to Europe while seeking entry to the Common Market (P nf). Europe built Ariane after the United States agreed to launch the Franco-German Symphonie satellites (1974-75) only on condition that they not be used commercially (P nf): dependence shown in practice is what moved governments.
*Industry policy and autonomy.* France's phrase was "independent access to space"; Japan bought American Delta technology under licence (1969 agreement) and replaced it step by step; India framed its programme as development: Vikram Sarabhai's often-quoted line is "We do not have the fantasy of competing with the economically advanced nations in the exploration of the moon or the planets or manned space-flight" (C\*, re-check before quoting).

**4.2 What made them cut.** *NASA, 1967-73:* outlays fell from about $5.9 billion (FY1966) to about $3.3 billion (FY1974) (P nf); the race was won, Vietnam and domestic programmes competed, and polls had never shown majority support for the spending [24]. Saturn V production was stopped in 1968, before the first landing; three Apollo missions were cancelled in 1970; NERVA went in January 1973 [61]. *Britain, 1971:* a small budget (about £3 million a year), no military customer (the deterrent had been bought from the United States), and an American rocket that could do the job for less [15]. *USSR, 1974:* four failures, a race already lost, and a new chief (Glushko) who had opposed the design from the start; the money went to Energia-Buran, justified as an answer to the Shuttle's supposed military uses [31][29] (P nf for the motive). *Europe's Hermes:* approved 1987, dropped 1992 after its weight and cost grew (crew escape provisions added after Challenger), German reunification squeezed budgets and Russian cooperation became available (P nf). *Brazil:* refused technology under the MTCR, funded thinly for twenty years, then lost 21 of its specialists in one explosion (2003); a joint venture with Ukraine was wound up in 2015 (P nf). Common pattern: **programmes die when the missile or prestige purpose is met or lost and no user is paying**; the survivors (Ariane, Soyuz, Long March, PSLV) had customers.

**4.3 Chief designers and rivalry.** *United States, 1955-58:* the Army (Redstone, Jupiter), Navy (Vanguard, Polaris) and Air Force (Atlas, Thor, Titan) each ran a rocket; the Defense Secretary's ruling of November 1956 limited the Army to short range; Thor and Jupiter duplicated each other; the satellite went to the least mature rocket [11]. The cure was organisational: ARPA (February 1958), NASA (October 1958), and the transfer of the Army's von Braun team to NASA on 1 July 1960 (C\*). *USSR:* the opposite course. A working council of chief designers under Korolev broke up over the N1's engines; Korolev, Chelomei and Yangel lobbied the leadership separately; there was no agency above them, and the result was two lunar programmes, three station lines and several heavy rockets at once. Siddiqi's conclusion is that the Soviet Union lost the Moon race through institutional disorder, not lack of talent or money [30] (P nf). One man's death mattered: Korolev's in 1966 has no American parallel. *Elsewhere:* Japan ran two agencies with two rocket families until they were merged in 2003 (C\*); China's programme was shielded from the Cultural Revolution only in part, and its first crewed project (1971) was cancelled within a year (P nf).

**4.4 Schedule pressure and accidents, in the inquiries' own words.** Rogers Commission (Challenger), chapter VIII, "Pressures on the System": "The capabilities of the system were stretched to the limit to support the flight rate in winter 1985/1986." and "In 1985, NASA published a projection calling for an annual rate of 24 flights by 1990." (C) [39]. Columbia Accident Investigation Board, organisational cause statement: "The organizational causes of this accident are rooted in the Space Shuttle Program's history and culture, including the original compromises that were required to gain approval for the Shuttle, subsequent years of resource constraints, fluctuating priorities, schedule pressures, mischaracterization of the Shuttle as operational rather than developmental, and lack of an agreed national vision for human space flight." and "In our view, the NASA organizational culture had as much to do with this accident as the foam." (C for the first clause and the second sentence, each found in two sources; the middle of the long sentence is from memory, C\*) [40]. Soviet cases with a date to meet: Sputnik 2 was built in under a month for the fortieth anniversary of the revolution, which is why Laika could not be brought back [56]; Nedelin had the R-16 worked on while fuelled, ahead of the same anniversary in 1960 [36]; Soyuz 1 flew with known faults before May Day 1967 (the detail that "203 faults" were reported and ignored comes from one disputed account: D).

**4.5 Secrecy and cover-up.** Soviet practice: failures unannounced; a neutral series name ("Kosmos", from March 1962) for military and failed flights alike; chief designers anonymous until death; a launch site named after a town 320 km away [21]; Nedelin's death given out as an air crash and admitted after 29 years [36]; the lunar programme denied for 20 [33]. American secrecy covered reconnaissance (the Corona programme was declassified in 1995, the agency running it acknowledged in 1992; C\*) but not launches: Vanguard exploded on live television. The consequences are the lever: secrecy protected prestige in the short run, produced the "phantom cosmonaut" rumours [70], and left Soviet engineers repeating mistakes; openness cost governments embarrassment and bought credibility.

**4.6 Cooperation as diplomacy.** Space cooperation tracked the political weather almost exactly: scientific exchange agreed in 1962; a joint flight agreed at the 1972 summit and flown in 1975; the framework allowed to lapse in 1982 after Afghanistan and Poland (P nf); Shuttle-Mir and the ISS as a way to support and bind post-Soviet Russia [59]; China kept out throughout. It also worked below the level of headlines: the US-Soviet-Canadian-French search-and-rescue satellite system (agreed 1979, first rescue 1982) operated through the worst years (C\*). Europe's rule of "fair return" (contracts in proportion to contributions) is what kept small members paying (C\*). Since 2020 there are two lunar camps, the Artemis Accords (76 signatories) and the Chinese-Russian lunar station project [48]; Russia's break with Europe in 2022 ended Soyuz launches from Kourou but not the ISS (C\*).
